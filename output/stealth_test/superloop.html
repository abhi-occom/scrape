    How devices can bottleneck your internet

    Old internet tech can really hold you back, no matter how fast your plan says it is. If your home’s wiring is stuck in the past, it’s like trying to pour a bucket of water through a tiny straw. Those ancient bits of tech can slow everything right down, causing buffering, lag, and frustration.

    What’s an nbn connection box?

    That little black box your nbn tech plugs in? That’s your connection box,or NTD (Network Termination Device). If you have an FTTP, FTTC, or HFC connection then the NTD is your business’ internet gateway. If you’re on a Fibre to the Node (FTTN) connection, then you’re still connected to the existing copper telephone wiring in your office and may not have an NTD. For Superloop customers, it’s the crucial link between your place and the high-speed nbn network.

    If you're looking to upgrade your speeds to ultrafast 2Gbps, you'll need nbn to install the latest connection box. It's a free upgrade, and we’ll arrange installation. And if you're on HFC, you'll install the latest connection box via the self install kit. It's a free upgrade and nbn will arrange delivery.

    Please note: This device is separate to your modem. If the connection box is not present there may be a delay in activation until nbn can dispatch a new connection box.

    Speed tiers indicate the theoretical maximum speed you’ll get to your home. Actual speeds you experience depend on a few things, like demand in other parts of the network, or in-home conditions (like the kids streaming and gaming all the things), as well as where you place your modem (putting your modem in the cupboard is a no no).     Typical Evening Speeds are the average speeds you can expect during the evening (specifically between 7pm and 11pm) from your internet.     About nbn speeds

    Depending on your specific address, different connection technologies can be used to connect your premises to the Superloop nbn network. Each connection type has a different theoretical maximum speed. In addition, within each technology, multiple speed tiers may be available. The table below explains the variety of nbn connection types.

    Connection technology

    Description

    Fibre to the Premises (FTTP)

    Optical Fibre runs from the internet and Superloop's network direct to your premises.

    Hybrid Fibre Coaxial (HFC)

    Optical Fibre runs from the internet and Superloop's network to a node in your street or nearby street. An existing ‘pay TV’ or coaxial cable then runs from the node to your premises.

    Fibre to the Node (FTTN)

    Optical Fibre runs from the internet and Superloop's network to a node in your street or nearby street. Copper wires (usually the existing phone line) runs from the node to your premises.

    Fibre to the Curb (FTTC)

    Optical Fibre runs from the internet and Superloop's network to a pit in the footpath close to your premises. Copper wires (usually the existing phone line) runs from the pit to your premises.

    Fibre to the Building (FTTB)

    Generally used in apartment blocks or similar type of buildings. Optical Fibre runs from the internet and Superloop's network to a node in your building's communications equipment room. A copper cable runs from the node to your premises.

    Fixed Wireless

    Typically used in circumstances where the distance between premises is large. A fixed antenna on your roof receives a signal from a wireless tower.

    Satellite

    A satellite dish is installed on your premises and receives a signal from a satellite.

    Several factors specific to your access line can impact your internet speeds, including:

    • The type of technology used (FTTN, FTTB, HFC, or Fixed Wireless)

    • The length and quality of the copper or coaxial line from your premises to the node

    • The condition of your internal cabling and the layout within your home

    • External electrical interference, both inside and outside your property

    • Network conditions such as the Co-existence period

    • Weather conditions, including extreme heat, heavy rain, and any obstacles affecting signal strength for Fixed Wireless connections

    Each of these factors can influence your overall internet performance, so it's important to consider them when assessing your connection speed.

    Co-existence refers to the period during which your nbn FTTN/B/C service, which partly relies on the copper network, operates alongside other services using the same copper line from your local exchange to your premises. These co-existing services include:

    • ADSL, ADSL2, ADSL2+

    • SHDSL

    • ISDN

    • Any other Copper ULL based service other than VDSL

    The Co-existence period starts when you can first access an nbn FTTN/B/C service and continues until all legacy services are disconnected from your local exchange.

    During this period, the presence of legacy services can impact the performance of your nbn FTTN/B/C service in the following ways:

    • Cross talk

    • Downstream power back-off applied by nbn

    • Other temporary measures taken by nbn to manage interactions between the performance of your nbn FTTN/B/C service and Co-existing services.

    NBN Co considers your nbn FTTN/B/C service within specification if, during the Co-existence period, your speed is above 12Mbps down and 1Mbps up, and after the Co-existence period ends, speeds are above 25Mbps down and 5Mbps up, regardless of the speed tier ordered.

    While Superloop strives to deliver the highest speed possible, factors outside of our control can affect performance. We always recommend using the Superloop supplied modem for nbn FTTN/B/C services to ensure you get the best possible speeds and support.

    During busy times, broadband speeds can drop as more people use the network at once. In these moments, the overall network capacity tends to have a greater impact on your speed than your chosen access line or speed tier.

    To handle congestion, Superloop uses tools to prioritise certain types of traffic. For example, during peak usage, we may prioritise voice calls (VoIP) and video streaming data, which require stable speeds to work well, over general browsing or downloading. Activities like software updates and file-sharing, which can overload the network, are typically given lower priority during these times.

    Your internet speed can also be affected by where the content you're accessing is hosted. If the server you're downloading from is congested or has limited capacity, it can cause delays, even with a fast home connection. Additionally, if the server is overseas, international network congestion could slow things down further.

    Carrier-Grade Network Address Translation (CGNAT) is a technique used to share one public IP address across multiple customer internet connections. This helps internet providers make more efficient use of their available IPv4 address pool.
